## Motivation
Our lives do not happen by chance. Instead, they are defined by a combination of the choices we make and the resources provided by those others around us.  For example, we decide when to get out of bed, whether to have kids, and what route to take to work, and when making these decisions we might factor in our financial situation and our past life experiences. 

Perhaps more importantly, we make perceptions regarding ourselves — for ex: how smart we perceive ourselves to be — which lead to a variety of choices like how much effort we choose to put into tasks or what job we pursue. These inner self-conceptions affect the way we present ourselves to the world, which probably affects how others interact with us. If they think we are smarter, other people might be more likely to provide resources as they think we will be more likely to succeed. 

## Contribution

In this paper, I ask how
self-perception impacts later life outcomes. Specifically, I focus on the
metric of confidence during grade school years and how varying levels of confidence is correlated with later
life outcomes. Using longitudinal data from the PSID, I designed the experiment, created metrics for over- and under- confidence, and explored their different impacts along gender lines. In other words,
how might an over-confidence effect impact aspects like education level or income in men differently than women?

To see my methodology & what I learnt, [read the full paper here!](/Final%20Econ%20Confidence%20Paper.pdf) :)
