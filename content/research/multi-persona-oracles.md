## The Gap in Modern Fairness
Algorithms have quietly embedded themselves into the fabric of daily life. They help determine whose résumé
is reviewed by a recruiter, who qualifies for a loan, how long someone remains incarcerated, and which patients
receive urgent care. Once considered neutral tools—efficient, data-driven, impartial—they are now recognized
as powerful arbiters of opportunity and harm.

How, then, can we ensure that these discretized methods are fair? What does fair even mean?

My thesis, which you can read in full here, sought to answer these questions.

Much like RLHF, a lot of modern-day fairness theory presumes the existence of a perfectly fair human judge, who can assess pairwise comparisons to show algorithms how to handle situations. However, it seems not only impractical for this fair human to be able to provide feedback efficiently on so many situations, but fairness seems more accurately defined as a pluralistic value rather than an entity with a ground truth.
